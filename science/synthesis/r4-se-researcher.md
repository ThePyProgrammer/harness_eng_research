## Reviewer 4: The SE Researcher (Software Engineering Venue Veteran)

### Summary

This paper proposes a unified formal framework ("seven pillars") for reasoning about AI coding agent harness architecture, drawing on information theory, reliability theory, control theory, computability theory, and lattice theory. Each pillar addresses one dimension (Abstraction, Information, Reliability, Coordination, Temporal, Quality, Governance), and cross-pillar synthesis identifies shared structural principles. The ambition is commendable, and the paper is clearly the product of sustained intellectual effort. However, the paper suffers from a fundamental tension between its aspirational scope and its empirical grounding: it presents formal machinery of considerable sophistication applied to a domain where almost none of the key parameters have been measured, and where several of the "novel" contributions are repackagings of well-known SE concepts under new terminology.

### Strengths

- **Breadth of synthesis.** Bringing together reliability theory (Young-Daly), concurrency control (isolation levels), submodular optimization, control theory (cascade loops, Nyquist), survival analysis, and lattice theory into a single coherent treatment of a practical engineering problem is genuinely valuable. The cross-pillar theorem index (Appendix A) is a useful artifact.

- **Honest self-assessment of evidence quality.** Section 10 (Empirical Foundations) and Table 7 (evidence quality assessment) are refreshingly candid. The authors explicitly flag six categories of claims with "Very Low" evidence quality and acknowledge that several key quantities are unmeasured. This level of intellectual honesty is rare and welcome.

- **The verification roadmap (Section 12) with explicit falsification criteria.** Each of the five proposed experiments specifies what would constitute falsification, including quantitative thresholds. This transforms the paper from pure theory into a testable research program.

- **The structural enforcement principle.** The observation that $(1-\epsilon)^T$ decays exponentially, independently derived across four pillars, is a genuinely useful design insight with clear practical implications. The connection to the METR reward-hacking data (43x amplification when scoring functions are visible) is compelling.

- **The Governance Information Budget (Proposition 9.1).** Unifying five pillar constraints into simultaneous inequalities on the change rate $\lambda$ is a clean formulation that identifies which pillar is the binding constraint for a given configuration. This is the kind of cross-cutting insight that justifies the multi-pillar approach.

- **Careful notation disambiguation.** The notation table in the introduction, acknowledging symbol reuse across pillars, is a small but important sign of care.

- **Engagement with contrarian positions (Section 13).** Steelmanning seven objections, including the Bitter Lesson, Suchman's situated action critique, and the Wittgensteinian challenge to fixed informational content, shows genuine philosophical seriousness.

### Weaknesses

- **FATAL: No empirical validation of the core formal claims.** The paper presents approximately 63 formal results across seven pillars. Of these, zero are validated by controlled experiment. The authors acknowledge this (Section 10.4, "The Calibration Gap"), but acknowledgment does not remedy the problem. For an SE venue, a paper that derives 63 theorems about software engineering practice without testing any of them against measured data is fundamentally incomplete. The verification roadmap is welcome but constitutes future work, not contribution. At minimum, one of the five proposed experiments should have been conducted.

- **FATAL: The "Accretion Category" claim of novelty is overstated.** The paper claims (Section 7.10, line 1426) that the Accretion Category "is absent from all classical defect taxonomies (IEEE 1044, ODC, Beizer, CWE) because all four assume intentional authorship." This is misleading on two counts. First, the concept of code that is individually correct but collectively harmful is well-established in the technical debt literature: Cunningham's original 1992 formulation, Kruchten et al.'s 2012 taxonomy of technical debt (design debt, architecture debt, code debt), and Fowler's code smells (especially "shotgun surgery" and "speculative generality") all describe patterns where individually defensible choices degrade system health. Second, the claim that classical taxonomies "assume intentional authorship" is a straw man; ODC's "assignment" and "checking" defect types, for instance, capture process failures that are orthogonal to author intent. The novelty is at best incremental: accretion describes an increased *rate* of a known phenomenon under a new generation mechanism, not a qualitatively new defect class.

- **MAJOR: The GitClear 8x duplication finding is used without adequate scrutiny of confounders.** The paper cites the 8x increase in 5+ line duplicated code blocks as a primary motivation (Introduction, line 94) and calibration point (Quality pillar, Governance pillar). However, the GitClear study is observational with no causal identification. Alternative explanations include: (a) the shift toward microservices and template-based project scaffolding that independently increases duplication; (b) selection effects in which projects adopt AI tools; (c) the denominator effect (if total LOC increased, duplication rate denominators shift); (d) changes in development practice (more copy-paste from Stack Overflow, independent of AI). The paper acknowledges "observational (no causal identification)" in Section 10.1 but then proceeds to use the 8x figure as if it were a causal estimate throughout. An SE venue would require at minimum a difference-in-differences or regression discontinuity design to attribute this change to AI code generation.

- **MAJOR: The DORA 2025 data is survey-based, and the paper overinterprets correlations as mechanisms.** The DORA finding that "software delivery stability showed a negative correlation with AI usage" is cited to support multiple pillar claims. But DORA's methodology combines self-reported survey data with telemetry, and the "negative correlation" is exactly that: a correlation. The paper's interpretation that this reflects compound error amplification or governance capacity overflow is plausible but unsupported by the DORA data itself, which does not decompose the stability effect into mechanisms. The claim that "bug rates up 9%" could "not be independently verified from the primary DORA report" (line 975) is concerning; citing unverifiable statistics undermines credibility.

- **MAJOR: The decidability classification applies Rice's theorem incorrectly in several places.** Rice's theorem states that every non-trivial semantic property of programs is undecidable. The paper uses this to classify slop types into D/SD/U categories (Table 2). However, the classification conflates two distinct problems: (a) deciding whether a given piece of code exhibits a defect, and (b) deciding whether a given piece of code is semantically equivalent to a simpler alternative. For the "Decidable" category, the paper claims hallucinated imports are decidable via "lockfile resolution ($O(1)$ table lookup)." This is correct for *syntactic* presence of an import not in the lockfile, but the *semantic* question "is this import unnecessary?" is undecidable by Rice's theorem. The classification is useful as engineering guidance but should not be presented as a formal decidability result grounded in computability theory. The invocation of Rice's theorem is a category error when applied to approximate, heuristic detection methods that never claim to solve the decision problem exactly.

- **MAJOR: The 18 slop types taxonomy lacks validation.** The taxonomy is presented as a contribution but is not validated against any labeled dataset, inter-rater reliability study, or comparison with existing taxonomies. How were these 18 types identified? Through systematic analysis of a corpus? Through practitioner interviews? Through the authors' personal experience? The paper does not say. IEEE 1044 went through a multi-year standardization process with industry input. ODC was validated across 25,000+ projects (Capers Jones). Beizer's taxonomy was grounded in decades of testing practice. The 18 slop types appear to be the authors' own ad hoc categorization, presented with the authority of a formal classification but none of the empirical grounding. At minimum, this requires: (a) a labeled dataset of AI-generated code defects, (b) inter-rater reliability measurement ($\kappa$), (c) coverage analysis against IEEE 1044 and ODC, and (d) exclusion analysis (are there AI code defects not captured by any of the 18 types?).

- **MAJOR: Conway's Law formalization (Theorem 5.8) adds minimal value over the original insight.** Formalizing Conway's Law as $G_R \subseteq G_T$ (the required communication graph must be a subgraph of the topology communication graph) is a graph-theoretic restatement of what Conway said in prose in 1968. The "Mismatch Cost" equation sums coupling weights over uncovered edges, but this is simply a weighted count of Conway violations. The paper claims this yields a "theorem" but the content is tautological: if agents that need to coordinate cannot communicate, coordination fails. MacCormack et al. (2012) already validated the mirroring hypothesis empirically; Nagappan et al. (2008) showed organizational metrics predict failure-proneness at 85% precision. The graph-theoretic formulation does not generate new predictions beyond what these empirical studies already established. The Inverse Conway for Agents (Theorem 5.9) reducing to balanced min-cut is more interesting but is essentially a relabeling of the standard module assignment problem.

- **MINOR: The paper lacks a proper Threats to Validity section.** For an SE venue, this is a significant structural omission. The Limitations section (Section 12) partially substitutes, but it does not follow the standard construct/internal/external/conclusion validity framework. Key threats that should be discussed explicitly include: (a) *construct validity*: the operationalization of "abstraction gap" via Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, and the Shannon substitute is model-dependent; (b) *internal validity*: the observational data sources (GitClear, DORA) have uncontrolled confounders; (c) *external validity*: all calibration data comes from a narrow set of tools, models, and benchmarks that may not generalize; (d) *conclusion validity*: the framework makes strong claims from limited data points (e.g., the "Fresh Eyes" 17.1% figure is a single study used to calibrate an exponential decay model).

- **MINOR: Kolmogorov complexity as a foundation introduces persistent uncomputability that weakens practical claims.** The Abstraction pillar defines the core quantity $\mathcal{G}(S,P) = K(P|S)$, acknowledges it is uncomputable, then switches to Shannon entropy $H(P|S)$ as the operational measure. This two-step is honest but unsatisfying: the clean theoretical results (Spec-Code Convergence, monotonicity, additivity) hold for $K$ but not necessarily for $H$ with tight bounds. The Shannon version "holds with exact equality" but over a distribution that is itself model-dependent ($\theta$). The paper should be more explicit about where the Kolmogorov results provide actionable guidance versus where they are aspirational.

- **MINOR: The "thinker placement" table (Section 2.6) is arbitrary and not falsifiable.** Placing Dijkstra at $\hat{\sigma} \approx 0.95$ and Lamport at $\hat{\sigma} \approx 0.78$ is a qualitative ordinal judgment presented with misleading numerical precision. The remark acknowledging "ordinal estimates" does not fully mitigate the problem; presenting two-decimal-place coordinates for quantities derived from uncomputable functions invites readers to take the numbers literally.

- **MINOR: The paper is extremely long (~3683 lines of LaTeX) and would benefit from aggressive pruning.** Seven pillars, each with 4-8 theorems, plus cross-pillar synthesis, empirical assessment, 35 design principles, seven contrarian engagements, a verification roadmap, and five appendices is far too much for a single paper. The contribution would be stronger as a focused paper on 2-3 pillars with actual empirical validation, rather than a panoramic survey with none.

### Detailed Comments

**Relation to existing SE literature.** The paper cites Perry and Wolf (1992) nowhere, despite their foundational work on software architecture being directly relevant to the architecture erosion concerns in the Governance pillar. Murphy's reflexion models are cited in the Related Work but not engaged substantively in the Governance formalism itself; the governance capacity bound (Proposition 8.1) would benefit from explicit comparison with the reflexion model's convergences/divergences/absences framework. Kruchten's technical debt taxonomy (2012) is absent entirely, despite the Accretion Category being a direct competitor to Kruchten's "design debt" and "architecture debt" categories. Fowler's refactoring work is cited only in passing despite the refactoring ratio being a key metric. The paper should engage with the extensive literature on architecture erosion and recovery, including Hochstein and Lindvall's work on architecture conformance checking, and the work by Passos et al. on architectural drift detection in evolving systems.

**The GitClear data.** The 8x figure specifically refers to "code blocks with five or more duplicated lines," which is a specific clone detection threshold. The paper should note that clone detection thresholds are arbitrary and that different thresholds yield different magnitudes. Additionally, the shift from 8.3% to 12.3% overall duplication rate (a 1.48x increase) is dramatically less alarming than the 8x figure for 5+ line blocks, suggesting that the 8x headline number may reflect a distributional shift in clone sizes rather than a fundamental increase in duplication. The paper should present both numbers with equal prominence.

**The DORA 2025 data.** The paper claims (line 975) that "naive AI-assisted parallelism frequently yields QAS < 1" and cites DORA data showing "review time up 91%, PR size up 154%." These are secondary interpretations of DORA data; the primary DORA report should be cited precisely. The DORA methodology involves self-selection (organizations that adopt AI tools may differ systematically from those that do not), and the negative correlation between AI usage and stability may reflect reverse causation (struggling teams adopt AI tools as a remedy, not AI tools causing instability).

**The 18 slop types.** The comparison with existing defect taxonomies is weak. IEEE 1044 provides a multi-attribute classification (activity, trigger, impact, mode); ODC provides 8 orthogonal defect types with validated development-phase correlations; Beizer provides 10 categories grounded in testing theory. The 18 slop types are neither shown to be orthogonal, nor mapped to existing taxonomies, nor validated for coverage or exclusivity. The three "generative factors" (Context Blindness, Completion Bias, Training Distribution Leakage) are interesting but presented as definitions rather than as empirically derived factors. A factor analysis on a labeled defect corpus would be the appropriate methodology.

**The decidability classification.** The paper should distinguish more carefully between decidability-in-principle (what Rice's theorem says) and detectability-in-practice (what tools can approximate). The "Semi-decidable" category is particularly problematic: in computability theory, semi-decidable (recursively enumerable) has a precise meaning (a Turing machine halts on positive instances), but the paper uses it informally to mean "partially detectable by heuristic methods." This conflation damages the formal credibility of the classification.

**Conway's Law formalization.** The graph containment condition $G_R \subseteq G_T$ is clean but trivially satisfied in practice by adopting a complete communication topology (mesh), which the paper acknowledges introduces $O(k^2)$ overhead. The interesting question is not whether the containment holds but what the optimal topology is given coupling structure, which is the balanced min-cut problem. The paper should lead with the optimization problem rather than the containment theorem.

**Threats to validity.** The paper should include an explicit threats section addressing:
- *Construct validity*: All core quantities ($K(P|S)$, codebase entropy $H(\mathcal{C})$, governance capacity $C_{\text{gov}}$) are either uncomputable or lack operational definitions with validated measurement procedures.
- *Internal validity*: The framework rests on observational data with known confounders (GitClear, DORA) and small-sample expert evaluations (METR with 4 evaluators).
- *External validity*: Calibration data comes from specific model families (Claude, GPT, Gemini), specific benchmarks (SWE-bench, HumanEval), and specific tools (Cursor, Codex, GSD). Generalization to different programming languages, application domains, and team contexts is unestablished.
- *Conclusion validity*: Many quantitative predictions (optimal context budget, optimal agent count, governance bifurcation threshold) depend on parameters that have not been measured. The sensitivity of conclusions to parameter uncertainty is not analyzed.

### Questions for Authors

1. Of the 63 formal results, which three would you prioritize for empirical validation, and what is the minimum viable experiment for each? (The verification roadmap proposes five; I am asking for a triage.)

2. How does the Accretion Category differ from Kruchten et al.'s "design debt" in the technical debt taxonomy? Both describe individually defensible design choices that collectively degrade system quality. If the difference is the generation mechanism (statistical completion vs. intentional choice), does that warrant a new defect class, or merely a new subcategory within existing technical debt frameworks?

3. The paper uses Rice's theorem to ground the decidability classification, but Rice's theorem applies to the *extensional* properties of programs (input-output behavior). Many of the "semi-decidable" slop types (over-engineering, architectural erosion, god functions) are properties of code *structure*, not behavior. Does Rice's theorem actually apply here, or would structural complexity theory (e.g., Blum's speedup theorem, or more pragmatically, the theory of code metrics) be a more appropriate formal foundation?

4. The governance capacity bound (Proposition 8.1) is described as a "structural analogy" to Shannon's channel coding theorem. How tight is this analogy? Shannon's theorem requires stationary ergodic sources and memoryless channels. Architectural drift is neither stationary nor ergodic (it accelerates as coupling increases), and the governance "channel" has memory (previous corrections affect future drift). Does the analogy survive these violations, or does it reduce to the qualitative observation that finite corrective bandwidth exists?

5. The Spec-Code Convergence Theorem applies to incompressible programs, but the paper immediately notes that real programs are highly compressible (Hindle et al., 1.25 bits per token). For the common case of compressible programs, what does the theorem actually predict? Is there a version that gives non-trivial bounds for typical compression ratios?

6. The paper proposes VIH (Verified Iterations per Hour) as a key metric but acknowledges (footnote, line 1038) that it has never been measured in any production harness. Given that the Temporal pillar derives multiple theorems about VIH optimization, how confident are you that VIH is the right metric, as opposed to (say) verified features per sprint, or verified diff quality per hour?

7. The three "generative factors" (Context Blindness, Completion Bias, Training Distribution Leakage) are presented as "approximately orthogonal." What evidence supports this orthogonality claim? Have you measured the mutual information between factor loadings on a corpus, or is "approximately orthogonal" an untested assumption?

8. Several cross-pillar connections (Section 9.5, paragraphs 1-5) are described as interactions that "merit formal development" but are not developed. If these are important enough to highlight as "missed connections," why were they not developed? This feels like the paper is advertising its own incompleteness.

9. The paper draws on blog posts and industry reports (HumanLayer, Spotify Honk, Cursor) as evidence. How should readers assess the reliability of these sources, given that they are produced by organizations with commercial interests in the tools being described?

10. The cost-of-quality model (Section 7.12) recalibrates Juran's 1:10:100 ratio by decidability class but does not cite the extensive empirical literature challenging the 1:10:100 ratio itself (e.g., Shull et al. 2002, Boehm and Basili 2001). Is the recalibration meaningful if the baseline ratio is disputed?

### Overall Assessment

**Score: 4/10** (below the borderline of 6)

**Confidence: 4/5** (I am very familiar with the SE literature on technical debt, code quality, defect taxonomies, architecture erosion, and empirical SE methodology. I am moderately familiar with the information-theoretic and reliability-theoretic machinery used.)

The paper is intellectually ambitious and contains several insights that would be publishable with proper empirical grounding (the structural enforcement principle, the governance information budget, the compound error sensitivity analysis). However, the current version is a theory paper that claims to be founding a "science" while providing zero empirical validation. The SE community has learned, through decades of experience, that formal models of software engineering practice must be validated against measured data to be useful. Lehman himself, whose laws of software evolution underpin the Governance pillar, spent decades collecting longitudinal data. The authors should do the same before claiming to establish a science.

The novelty claims around the Accretion Category and the slop taxonomy are overstated relative to existing technical debt and defect classification literature. The use of Rice's theorem is imprecise in several places. The paper's length is a barrier to reviewing and, more importantly, to impact: a focused paper with one or two validated pillars would be far more influential than a panoramic unvalidated framework.

### Recommendation

**Weak Reject.**

The paper should be substantially revised before resubmission. Specific recommendations:

1. **Conduct at least one of the five proposed experiments** (preferably the context budget experiment, which the authors estimate at 2-4 months and $5-15K). A single validated prediction would transform the paper from speculation to science.

2. **Reduce scope to 3-4 pillars** and develop them with empirical calibration. The remaining pillars can be referenced as future work.

3. **Engage seriously with the technical debt literature** (Cunningham 1992, Kruchten et al. 2012, Brown et al. 2010, Li et al. 2015) and position the Accretion Category within it, rather than claiming it is absent from "all classical defect taxonomies."

4. **Add a proper Threats to Validity section** following the construct/internal/external/conclusion framework standard in empirical SE.

5. **Tighten the decidability classification** by distinguishing between formal decidability (Rice's theorem) and practical detectability (tool capabilities). The current conflation undermines the formal rigor the paper aspires to.

6. **Cite Perry and Wolf (1992), Kruchten et al. (2012), and Passos et al.** on architecture erosion. Engage with the reflexion model literature substantively, not just in the Related Work.
