## Reviewer 1: The Empiricist (Senior ML Researcher)

### Summary

This paper proposes a unified formal framework for AI coding agent harness architecture, organized around seven "pillars" (Abstraction, Information, Reliability, Coordination, Temporal, Quality, Governance) with information theory as the claimed unifying currency. The paper applies established mathematical machinery (Kolmogorov complexity, Shannon entropy, reliability theory, control theory, lattice theory, survival analysis) to the harness design problem and derives 35 design principles. The authors are commendably honest about the empirical gaps, but the gap between the formal apparatus and any experimental validation is so wide that the paper reads more as an intellectually stimulating manifesto than as a contribution to science.

### Strengths

- **Impressive breadth and ambition.** Synthesizing seven architectural dimensions into a single framework with shared formal vocabulary is a genuinely useful organizing contribution. The cross-pillar connections (Section 9) are the strongest part of the paper; the Governance Information Budget (Equation 14) and the three cascades (abstraction gap chain, compound error cascade, structural enforcement principle) are insightful.

- **Unusual intellectual honesty.** The paper self-identifies its empirical weaknesses with a level of candor rarely seen. Table 8 (Evidence Quality Assessment) explicitly labels six of its own claims as "Very Low" evidence quality. The contrarian engagement (Section 12) is substantive, not performative, particularly the Suchman/Wittgenstein critique of the information-theoretic foundations.

- **Strong calibration awareness.** The paper carefully distinguishes between parameters derived from data (GitClear's 211M lines, Kim et al.'s 180 configurations) and parameters assumed or estimated. The concrete verification roadmap (Section 12.2) with explicit falsification criteria is a model for how theory papers should specify their empirical debts.

- **Practical relevance.** The 35 design principles (Section 11) are tiered by implementation effort, grounded in specific theorems, and many are immediately actionable. The principle "Budget context, do not maximize it" with the supporting Context Budget Theorem is a good example of theory producing non-obvious, useful guidance.

- **Notation disambiguation.** The paper explicitly flags symbol reuse ($\delta$, $\gamma$, $\kappa$, $\sigma$, $n$ all overloaded) in a disambiguation table. This is necessary but rarely done.

- **The Accretion Category concept.** Identifying "code that is correct, unnecessary, individually defensible, and collectively harmful" as a novel defect class absent from all classical taxonomies (IEEE 1044, ODC, Beizer, CWE) is a genuinely original conceptual contribution, well-motivated by the GitClear data.

### Weaknesses

- **(FATAL) No original experiments.** This is a 3,683-line paper that claims to present a "science" for harness architecture, yet it reports zero original experimental results. Every empirical claim is borrowed from secondary sources (GitClear, DORA, Kim et al., METR, Liu et al., Chroma, etc.). A science requires experiments that test the theory's predictions; this paper has none. The verification roadmap in Section 12.2 is laudable, but it is a promissory note, not a contribution. Five proposed experiments, zero completed, is not a science; it is a research proposal.

- **(FATAL) Unfalsifiable core claims.** Several central theorems are either mathematical identities (Compound Error Sensitivity: $\mathcal{E}(R,p) = n$ is a direct differentiation result), standard textbook results applied to a new domain (the Shannon chain rule decomposition of the abstraction gap), or rely on uncomputable quantities (the Abstraction Gap $K(P|S)$ is uncomputable by the authors' own admission). The Spec-Code Convergence Theorem (Theorem 2.1) is true by elementary Kolmogorov complexity theory for incompressible programs, but the authors immediately note that most real programs are highly compressible, substantially weakening the theorem's relevance. The Governance Capacity Bound (Theorem 8.1) is framed as a "structural analogy" to Shannon's channel coding theorem, not a direct application; the authors acknowledge that the "governance channel" lacks the stationary transition probabilities and finite input alphabet required by Shannon's theorem. This means the bound is a metaphor, not a theorem.

- **(MAJOR) Calibration data does not support the precision of the claims.** The paper claims optimal context budgets of "$|C^*| \approx 0.15W$ to $0.40W$" but this is derived from model fits to degradation data from a handful of studies, not from direct optimization experiments. The degradation function $\delta(n) = (n/W_{\text{eff}})^{\alpha_d}$ with $\alpha_d \in [0.3, 0.5]$ is fit to three data points from a single model (Llama-3.1-70B) in Appendix B.7. Three data points from one model do not support a claimed universal optimal range. Similarly, the "Thinker Placement" table assigning $(\hat{\sigma}, \hat{\kappa})$ coordinates to Dijkstra, Hoare, Lamport, Knuth, and Brooks is presented with decimal precision but is acknowledged to be "ordinal estimates based on published positions, not computed quantities." This creates a veneer of quantitative precision over what is fundamentally qualitative judgment.

- **(MAJOR) "Proof sketches" are not proofs.** Of the approximately 25 theorems and propositions in the paper, essentially all are accompanied by "proof sketches" rather than complete proofs. Several of these sketches are hand-wavy at critical steps. For example, the proof sketch for submodularity of the context relevance function (Section 3.1) lists three "sufficient conditions" (conditional independence, joint Gaussianity, log-supermodularity) without verifying that any holds for actual LLM-based code generation. The proof sketch for the Governance Bifurcation Theorem (Theorem 8.7) performs a fixed-point analysis that is standard but does not verify the structural assumptions of the dynamical system (is $\beta > 1$ empirically justified? Is the drift term truly linear in $(1-C)$?). The paper positions itself as a formal framework, but the formalism is incomplete.

- **(MAJOR) The information-theoretic unification is weaker than claimed.** The authors acknowledge (Section 9.1) that the information-theoretic framing is "valid but retrospective" for the execution axis (Reliability, Coordination, Temporal), where "the results would stand unchanged without the information-theoretic gloss." This is a significant concession: the claimed unifying currency is genuinely unifying for 4 of 7 pillars, and decorative for the other 3. Calling compound errors "information destruction" does not add explanatory power; it is relabeling. A more honest framing would present two related frameworks (an information-theoretic framework for the semantic and assurance axes, and a reliability/scheduling framework for the execution axis) rather than claiming a single unified theory.

- **(MAJOR) The empirical sources bear disproportionate weight and have significant limitations.** The paper's own Table 7 reveals concentration risk: GitClear, DORA, and METR each support claims across 3-4 pillars. GitClear (211M lines) is observational with no causal identification from a single platform. DORA 2025 includes survey-based components and measures correlation, not causation. METR used 4 evaluators across 3 repositories, a sample that provides moderate confidence at best. Kim et al. 2025 used benchmark tasks, not production code. A successful methodological challenge to any one of these four sources would ripple across the entire framework.

- **(MAJOR) VIH has never been measured.** The paper proposes Verified Iterations per Hour as a central metric of the Temporal pillar and uses it extensively in design principles, yet the footnote on Definition 6.2 states: "VIH has not, to our knowledge, been measured in any production harness. It is proposed here as a metric, not reported as an empirical finding." A science that centers on a metric nobody has ever measured is a science of hypotheticals.

- **(MINOR) The "18 slop types" taxonomy lacks validation.** The Quality pillar's classification of 18 defect types into 3 decidability classes is conceptually clean but entirely unvalidated. No labeled dataset exists. No inter-rater reliability study has been conducted. The three-factor latent structure (Context Blindness, Completion Bias, Training Distribution Leakage) is proposed as "approximately orthogonal" but has never been tested via confirmatory factor analysis. The paper acknowledges this but proceeds to build an NP-hardness result and a greedy approximation algorithm on top of this unvalidated taxonomy.

- **(MINOR) Overloaded notation despite the disambiguation table.** The paper reuses $\delta$, $\gamma$, $\kappa$, $\sigma$, and $n$ across pillars with different meanings. The disambiguation table helps, but the paper is still difficult to read when cross-pillar results reference shared symbols. For example, $\gamma$ means common-cause failure rate in the Reliability pillar and drift propensity in the Governance pillar, yet both appear in the cross-pillar synthesis.

- **(MINOR) The title oversells.** "Towards a Science" implies empirical validation is either present or imminent. The paper is more accurately described as "Towards a Formal Framework," which is still a substantial contribution but sets different expectations.

### Detailed Comments

**On the gap between formal models and empirical validation.** This is the paper's central weakness, and the authors know it. Section 10 (Empirical Foundations) and Section 12.1 (Empirical Validation Gaps) are admirably candid. But candor about the gap does not close it. The paper contains approximately 63 formal results (per the Appendix A index), of which 19 participate in cross-pillar arguments. Of these 63 results, how many have been empirically tested? Essentially zero by the authors, and at most a handful have indirect support from secondary sources. The ratio of formal machinery to empirical validation is at least 10:1, probably higher. This is typical of "framework" papers in software engineering, which the ML community has learned to be skeptical of.

**On whether the calibration data supports the claims.** The paper's most cited empirical source, GitClear 2025, provides observational data on code quality metrics over time. It shows correlations (AI adoption coincides with increased duplication, decreased refactoring) but cannot establish causation. Multiple confounders exist: the same period saw increased open-source dependency adoption, increased microservice fragmentation, and changing developer demographics. The paper treats GitClear findings as evidence for specific formal claims (e.g., the Accretion Category, the refactoring ratio threshold) without acknowledging these confounders.

The METR 2026 study (50% of test-passing PRs would not be merged) is cited repeatedly, but the paper commendably notes the base rate: only 68% of human-written golden patches would be merged on re-review. The AI-attributable gap is thus ~18 percentage points, not 50. However, the paper then continues to cite "50%" in several places (e.g., in the circular validation bias discussion) without consistently using the adjusted 18-point figure.

**On the quality of "proof sketches" vs. full proofs.** The Governance Capacity Bound (Theorem 8.1) is the clearest example of a "proof by analogy" rather than a proof. The claim is that architectural drift exceeding governance capacity leads to unbounded divergence, by analogy to Shannon's channel coding theorem. But Shannon's theorem assumes a well-defined channel with stationary statistics, a precise codebook, and a clear notion of decoding error. None of these hold for the "governance channel." What is the input alphabet? What is the channel transition matrix? What is the error criterion? Without these, calling this a "theorem" is generous; it is a design heuristic phrased in mathematical notation. The authors' own Remark following the proof sketch acknowledges this: "The principle's value is in design guidance," which is precisely the right framing, but then the result should not be labeled a theorem.

The Governance Bifurcation Theorem (Theorem 8.7) is more rigorous: it performs a genuine fixed-point analysis of a specified ODE. But the ODE itself ($dC/dt = \mu G(1-C)^\beta - \gamma R(1-C)$) is assumed, not derived from first principles. Why is the governance term $(1-C)^\beta$ with $\beta > 1$? Why is the drift term linear in $(1-C)$? These structural assumptions determine the bifurcation result; different functional forms would produce different dynamics. The paper states the asymmetry "reflects the empirical observation that easy violations are caught first" but provides no data to support the specific functional form.

**On falsifiability.** Most theorems in the paper are either: (a) mathematical identities that are true by definition ($R = p^n$, the Shannon chain rule), (b) consequences of standard theory applied to a new domain (Spec-Code Convergence from Kolmogorov complexity), or (c) consequences of assumed functional forms (Governance Bifurcation from the assumed ODE). Category (a) cannot be falsified. Category (b) is falsifiable only insofar as the mapping to the new domain is correct (do agent pipeline steps really have independent failure probabilities? The paper itself doubts this via the common-cause model). Category (c) is falsifiable by showing the assumed functional form is wrong, but this requires the very empirical data the paper lacks. The paper's most genuinely falsifiable predictions are in the verification roadmap (Section 12.2), which specifies precise criteria. This is commendable. But none of these predictions have been tested.

**On the Limitations section.** Section 12 is well-written and identifies genuine gaps: human factors not modeled, uncomputability of idealized metrics, the theory preservation problem, scale-dependent governance ROI, and the Goodhart measurement problem. The verification roadmap (Section 12.2) is the best part of the paper after the cross-pillar synthesis. The falsification criteria are specific and testable. The resource estimates are realistic. If these experiments were actually run, this paper would be significantly stronger. As submitted, the roadmap demonstrates that the authors know what science looks like; they have not yet done it.

**On "science" vs. "framework."** The title claims "Towards a Science." A science requires: (1) formal models that make predictions, (2) experiments that test those predictions, (3) iteration between theory and experiment. This paper delivers (1) comprehensively and (3) as a roadmap. It delivers zero on (2). The paper is an excellent formal framework with a research agenda attached. It is not a science, and calling it one overstates the contribution. The closest analog in the ML literature is a "position paper with formal backing," which is a legitimate and valuable contribution, but should be evaluated on those terms rather than as empirical science.

### Questions for Authors

1. Of the 63 formal results indexed in Appendix A, which single result do you consider most likely to be falsified by empirical data, and why?

2. The Governance Capacity Bound is labeled a "theorem" but acknowledged as a "structural analogy" to Shannon's theorem. Could you specify the precise mathematical object that constitutes the "governance channel" (input alphabet, output alphabet, transition probabilities), or do you agree this result is more accurately described as a design principle?

3. The paper claims information theory is the "unifying currency" but concedes it is "decorative" (my word; your phrase is "retrospective") for 3 of 7 pillars. Would the paper be strengthened by dropping the unification claim and instead presenting two related frameworks?

4. VIH has never been measured. Have you attempted to instrument any production harness to measure it? If so, what prevented data collection? If not, why propose a metric you have not attempted to measure?

5. The degradation function $\delta(n)$ is fit to 3 data points from 1 model (Appendix B.7). What is the minimum number of models and data points you would need to have confidence in the power-law form and the $\alpha_d \in [0.3, 0.5]$ range?

6. The Accretion Category is a compelling concept. Have you attempted to build even a proof-of-concept detector? What would precision and recall look like against the GitClear dataset?

7. The paper claims the 18 slop types arise from three "approximately orthogonal" generative factors. What evidence supports orthogonality? Have you computed any correlations between factor loadings, even on a small labeled sample?

8. Several design principles (e.g., P1: "Budget context at 0.15W to 0.40W") are stated with apparent precision. Given that the underlying calibration is from a handful of studies, would it be more responsible to state these as qualitative guidelines ("substantially less than full context window") rather than numerical ranges?

9. The Compound Error Sensitivity theorem has elasticity $n$. You note that most failures cluster in problem framing (step 1). Does this mean the independence assumption underlying $R = p^n$ is so violated in practice that the theorem's quantitative predictions are unreliable, even though its qualitative message (compound errors are devastating) remains correct?

10. The paper engages seven contrarian positions but does not engage the most fundamental one from the ML community: that the entire enterprise of formal specification for LLM-generated code is misguided because LLMs operate in a distributional regime where worst-case complexity-theoretic bounds (Kolmogorov, Rice) are irrelevant. What is your response to the argument that average-case empirical performance is the only thing that matters, and that formal bounds on incompressible programs or undecidable properties are never binding in practice?

### Overall Assessment

**Score: 5/10** (below borderline accept)

**Confidence: 4/5** (high confidence; I have reviewed extensively in formal methods, ML systems, and software engineering)

This is an intellectually ambitious, well-organized, and unusually honest paper that presents a comprehensive formal framework for an important and timely problem. The cross-pillar synthesis is genuinely insightful. The design principles are practically useful. The verification roadmap is a model of how to specify empirical debts.

However, the paper's central claim, that it contributes to a "science" of harness architecture, is unsupported by the paper's own standard. A science requires experiments. This paper has none. The formal results are either standard mathematics applied to a new domain, consequences of assumed functional forms, or metaphors dressed in theorem-proof notation. The calibration data is borrowed, sparse, and observational. The core metric (VIH) has never been measured. The core taxonomy (18 slop types) has never been validated.

The paper would score substantially higher if it: (a) completed even one of the five proposed experiments, (b) validated the slop taxonomy on a labeled dataset, (c) measured VIH in a real harness, or (d) retitled itself as "A Formal Framework for AI Coding Agent Harness Architecture" and dropped the "science" framing. As submitted, it promises a science but delivers a framework.

### Recommendation

**Weak Reject**

The paper should be revised to either (a) include original empirical validation of at least one major theoretical prediction, or (b) reframe itself explicitly as a formal framework and position paper, with the "science" framing deferred to a follow-up that includes the proposed experiments. In its current form, the gap between the ambition of the title and the evidence in the paper is too large for acceptance at a top venue. I would encourage resubmission with empirical results.
